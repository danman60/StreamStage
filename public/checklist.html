The Recital Video + Livestream Checklist

Recital night is already the biggest shoot of your year. Part one is how you leave it with a season of content instead of one long recording. Part two is the interview questions — the ones that get real answers instead of a hostage video. Part three is everything to confirm before you promise families they can watch from anywhere. Part four is the brief to hand whoever actually shoots it — what to require, and the five questions to ask before you sign. In between, the content day planner is how one booked morning feeds a season of posts.

The routines are the easy part. What you will wish you had in August is the lobby, the applause, and the parents saying why they keep coming back.

Part one — the recital video

Before the night decide this while you can still change it

  • Name the deliverables out loud: full show, per-routine cuts, a highlight reel, vertical social cuts, next season’s registration film.
  • Confirm dancer and family photo/video consent, and how opt-outs will be handled in the edit.
  • Get the running order, act breaks, and correct spelling of every routine and teacher name — titles are where recital videos go wrong.
  • Decide where the camera positions live before the house is set, and reserve the seats or the riser.
  • Book the second shooter or roaming camera if you want anything beyond the stage.

Coverage on the night the footage you cannot go back for

  • Lock a clean, centred wide of the whole stage that runs continuously — this is the safety master.
  • Add closer angles for faces. A recital with no faces in it is a recording, not a film.
  • Shoot the room: the audience, the applause, a parent’s reaction, the reunion in the hallway afterward.
  • Shoot the lobby and backstage: hair, nerves, last checks, the huddle before the entrance.
  • Grab vertical clips deliberately — do not plan to crop landscape footage into a reel later.
  • Record ambient room sound as well as the board feed so applause survives the edit.

Interviews five prompts, asked of everyone

  • Set one lit corner with clean sound and bring people to it, rather than chasing them around the lobby.
  • Ask every subject the same short prompts so the answers cut together as a series.
  • Get parents as well as dancers. A parent explaining why they chose the studio outperforms any ad you will write.
  • Ask for one sentence that starts “If you’re thinking about signing your dancer up…”
  • Keep the takes short. Thirty usable seconds per person is a full season of posts.
  • The full question list, the provoke technique and the fast edit are in Part two.
  • Hiring someone to shoot it? Part four is the brief to hand them.

After the show turn one night into next season

  • Back the cards up twice, in two places, before anyone goes home.
  • Deliver the parent version first — permanent link, clear titles, easy to watch and download.
  • Cut a highlight reel while the night is still current, not in September.
  • Build the registration film: interviews + room energy + the best routine moments, ending in a call to action.
  • Slice the leftovers into dated social posts and schedule them across the summer.
  • Keep an organised master archive. Next year’s promo is made from this year’s footage.

Part one and a half — the content day planner

One morning in, twelve months of posts out. Recital night is the biggest shoot of your year, but it only happens once. A content day is the deliberate version: one booked morning, four stations, and a season of material that does not depend on the show.

What to prep before the day none of this is our job — it is the half that only you can do

  • Pick the date everyone can make — hair, makeup and costumes ready. Treat it like picture day, but for video.
  • List three hero routines and three people with a story worth telling. Not your six best dancers — three people who will actually talk.
  • Gather the brand bits — logo file, studio colours, and the phrases you always say. The ones you repeat are the ones that belong on screen.
  • Warn the building about the hazer. Ask us first — it is worth it, and it is the one thing that surprises a venue.
  • Block one morning. That is the whole ask.

What audiences actually watch public audit of studio, educator + competition accounts, July 2026

  • Mic’d-up class — corrections, effort, jokes, breath. The lesson as it actually sounds, which is access to the real experience rather than a highlight of it. See @joffreyballet.
  • Backstage POV — cues, nerves, rituals, costume checks, the entrance into light. The threshold before the stage is more watchable than the stage. See @imaginedancechallenge.
  • Day in the life — give the day a question worth answering, e.g. “can we get 40 dancers ready before doors?” The studio gets a protagonist. See @ikindance.
  • Correction → breakthrough — attempt, note, second attempt, payoff. Story structure for free, and it proves teaching instead of claiming it. See @theballeteducator.
  • Backstage interviews — five prompts, asked of everyone. One station, dozens of posts. People, not just performances. See @moveitshakeit.

These formats recur across unrelated public accounts. Watch time, saves and shares are not public — treat the pattern as a strong signal, not a guarantee.

Run it as stations one four-hour shoot, several setups

  • Interview / story station — the heart of it. People doing people things.
  • Selfie studio — fast and shareable, and the dancers make the content themselves.
  • Routines — the polished material. This is the brand reel.
  • Extra angles — every device is another focal length. Phones, a GoPro, a drone if the room allows it.

One clip, nine posts plan the cutdowns before you shoot, not after

  • Decide the outputs first: a reel, a recital cut, a teaser, a 15-second acro clip, a story, a slow-motion moment, a promo cut. Same footage, nine destinations.
  • Shoot each one deliberately. A vertical reel grabbed on purpose beats a landscape master cropped in a panic.
  • Date the posts and schedule them across the off-season so one night keeps working through the summer.

What you walk out with the point of booking the morning

  • A homepage story video — the one that makes a new parent pick you over the studio down the road.
  • A living background video — so your website stops looking like a brochure.
  • Recital and competition reels — same lights, same look, so the feed is consistent all year instead of a scrapbook.
  • Dozens of social cuts, scheduled — one clip becomes nine posts, dated and spread across the off-season.

Keep posting your own phone clips. The pro footage is the well you draw from, not a replacement for you. A 30-second edit can eat two to four hours of your night — that is the part worth handing over.

Part two — the interview questions

All the good stuff’s in the middle. Nobody wants the beginning of an answer and nobody wants the end. People warm up in their first sentence and wrap up in their last — the usable thirty seconds is in between. Shoot knowing that and you stop worrying about run time.

Everyone swap in your studio name · ask all of them the same ones

  • What do you love about [Studio]?
  • Why did you choose [Studio]?
  • Tell me about the culture at [Studio].
  • What’s changed since you started here?
  • Finish this sentence: “If you’re thinking about signing your dancer up…”

Parents

  • What did you notice at home that you didn’t expect?
  • What would you say to the parent who’s on the fence?
  • What’s a night here you’ll still be talking about in ten years?

Dancers

  • What can you do now that you couldn’t do last September?
  • Who’s the teacher you’d follow anywhere, and why?
  • What’s something you tried here that you’d never have tried anywhere else?

Teachers & owner

  • What do you want a dancer to walk out with that isn’t a trophy?
  • What’s the moment you knew this was working?
  • Get yourself in the chair. Most owners do not want to, and it is the most valuable minute of the day — nobody else feels about your studio the way you do, and a new parent can hear it.

The provoke the one technique that changes the footage

A straight question makes people careful, and careful people give you nothing. Ask the question, then hand them the opposite and let them argue with you. They will defend your studio harder than you ever could, and they will do it in their own words.

You: “What do you love about [Studio]? Is it a really fun place to be — or is everybody pretty miserable?”
Them: “Oh no, it’s a wonderful place. Everyone loves it here — my daughter cries when the season ends.”
That second sentence is your whole promo video. You would not have got it from the polite version of the question.

What failure sounds like the hostage video

  • Everyone answers “yes, I love [Studio], it’s a wonderful culture.” Flat, agreeable, useless — and you don’t find out until you’re in the edit with nothing to cut.
  • If the first two people give you that, change the question, not the person: provoke it, or ask for one specific night instead of a general feeling.
  • Let the silence sit. The pause after they think they have finished is where the real answer arrives.
  • Captions on every talking-head clip before it goes out.

Cut four hours of interviews in an evening free tools

  • DaVinci Resolve is free and it is what the pros use. Transcribe the whole shoot with its built-in AI transcription.
  • Paste the wall of text into ChatGPT and ask: “four 60-second scripts from the best material in here, verbatim, nothing added.”
  • It hands you the exact words back. Search that text inside Resolve and it jumps straight to the clip — you are editing by reading, not by scrubbing.
  • CapCut is a fine alternative and easier for captions.

Part three — the livestream

Venue + technical readiness

  • Test the venue’s actual upload speed from the camera position, not just the lobby Wi-Fi.
  • Confirm a dedicated, wired internet connection — or a bonded-cellular backup if Wi-Fi is unreliable.
  • Plan camera coverage: a clean, centred wide shot plus enough additional angles to make the performance watchable.
  • Walk the camera positions: sightlines, power, lighting changes, and a safe path for cables.
  • Confirm who can access the venue network, electrical outlets, and the house audio board on show day.

Audio that feels like the room

  • Take a tested feed from the house audio board for music, microphones, and announcements.
  • Add a room or audience microphone so applause, entrances, and the energy in the theatre do not disappear.
  • Monitor the board feed and room microphone together before doors open. A clean feed alone can feel sterile; a room mic alone can sound distant.

The family viewing experience

  • Decide what families receive: a livestream link, password or access code, replay window, and final recording.
  • Prepare one clear message with the link, start time, timezone, device tips, and a support contact.
  • Schedule reminders: one week before, the day before, and about 30 minutes before showtime.
  • Make sure the stream page is simple enough for grandparents to open on a phone, tablet, or smart TV.

Content + rights

  • Confirm music, platform, and archive expectations. A platform may mute or restrict copyrighted music without warning.
  • Define the show structure, privacy, access controls, dancer opt-outs, and whether chat is on or off before distributing the link.

After the show: the version families keep

  • Add clear on-screen titles: studio name, show title, date, and any helpful act or routine identifiers.
  • Deliver a permanent digital link to every parent, in a familiar format that is simple to watch and download.
  • Set the final-delivery date, where the video will live, and how families will receive it after the show.
  • Keep the locally recorded master as the safety net and final archival version if the livestream has an issue.

Part four — hand this to your videographer

Most recital video goes wrong in the booking conversation, not on the night. Give this part to whoever is shooting your recital — a local videographer, a company, or a parent with a good camera. You do not need to understand it; they will. All of it is normal to ask for, and if somebody tells you it is not, that is useful information about them.

Coverage what you cannot go back for

  • One clean, centred wide of the whole stage, running continuously — the safety master. Everything else is a bonus on top of it.
  • At least one closer angle for faces. A recital with no faces in it is a recording, not a film.
  • The room, not just the stage — audience, applause, a parent's reaction, the hallway afterward, and the lobby and backstage: hair, nerves, the huddle before the entrance.
  • Vertical clips grabbed deliberately, not cropped out of the landscape master later.
  • Open gate if the camera does it (the near-square sensor mode) — that is what turns one shot into both a landscape cut and a full-resolution vertical. 4K, 30fps unless they give you a reason, and a reason is fine.

Audio the half everyone forgets

  • A tested feed from the house board for music, mics and announcements.
  • Plus a room or audience mic, so applause survives the edit. A board feed alone sounds sterile; a room mic alone sounds distant. You want both.
  • Monitored together before doors open, not discovered afterward.

Backup non-negotiable

  • Cards backed up twice, in two places, before anyone leaves the building.
  • A local recording even if it is being livestreamed. The local file is the archive; the stream is the convenience.

Delivery speed and ease beat picture quality, once quality is acceptable

  • A permanent digital link — not a disc, not a USB key. Families lose the physical thing, and a scratch kills it.
  • A gallery split per routine, with thumbnails and correct titles: studio, show, date, routine, teacher. Titles are where recital videos go wrong.
  • Downloadable, not stream-only, so families keep their own copy — and every routine and teacher name spelled correctly. Send the running order in writing and hold them to it.
  • A stated delivery date, in days not seasons. Ten days is achievable; September is not acceptable for a June show.
  • A highlight reel, vertical social cuts, and next season's registration film — ask up front. They cost almost nothing at booking and a lot afterward.

The money model charge a media fee, stop selling discs

Nobody buys media by the object any more. Families are paying for the media to be created — so stop selling copies at the door and put a media fee on every dancer, the way you already handle costumes.

Ask your videographer: “what is your per-dancer rate?” → set your media fee above it → every family gets the link, you never chase a sale, and you never wonder whether the video made money.
Your fee minus their rate, times your dancer count, is your margin — decided in advance, in writing. No revenue share, no percentage split, no platform fee.

Five questions to ask before you sign

  • What is your turnaround, in days, from show night to the parent link?
    An evasive answer here is the whole answer.
  • How do families receive it, who hosts it, and for how long?
    “We’ll send a link” is not a plan.
  • Open gate — or how are you handling vertical?
    This is the difference between social content and just a show recording.
  • What is the per-dancer rate, and what is included at it?
    Reels, titles, backups, angles: named, not assumed.
  • Board feed and a room mic?
    The fastest way to find out whether they have done this before.

One more thing, while the cameras are already there

Recital night is peak vibes — everyone dressed up, everyone in the building, once. Ask for one lit corner and five questions asked of everyone, and that night becomes next season's promo instead of one long recording. The questions are in Part two, they are free, and they work with any videographer at all.

Shoot it once. Let it recruit for you all year.
