All the good stuff's in the middle. Nobody wants the beginning, nobody wants the end.

Build the station TEN MINUTES

A chair. A soft light on their face. A plant behind them, or one of your awards. A microphone on a stand or hung just out of frame — a cheap one beats the camera mic every time.

Your reception lobby is usually the best room in the building for this. Run it while the other three stations are running.

Who sits in the chair PICK SIX TO TEN

You already know who they are — the families who'd talk about your studio for an hour without being asked. Call them a week ahead and book them a time.

Get parents as well as dancers. A parent saying why they chose you outperforms any ad you'll ever write. And get yourself in the chair — nobody else feels about your studio the way you do.

The questions ASK EVERYONE THE SAME ONES — THEN THE ANSWERS CUT TOGETHER AS A SERIES

  1. EVERYONE
  2. What do you love about [Studio]?
  3. Why did you choose [Studio]?
  4. Tell me about the culture at [Studio].
  5. What's changed since you started here?
  6. Finish this sentence: "If you're thinking about signing your dancer up…"
  7. PARENTS
  8. What did you notice at home that you didn't expect?
  9. What would you say to the parent who's on the fence?
  10. What's a night here you'll still be talking about in ten years?
  11. DANCERS
  12. What can you do now that you couldn't do last September?
  13. Who's the teacher you'd follow anywhere, and why?
  14. What's something you tried here that you'd never have tried anywhere else?
  15. TEACHERS & OWNER
  16. What do you want a dancer to walk out with that isn't a trophy?
  17. What's the moment you knew this was working?

The provoke — the one technique that changes everything

A straight question makes people careful. So ask the question, then hand them the opposite and let them argue with you. They'll defend your studio harder than you ever could.

You: "What do you love about [Studio]? Is it a really fun place to be — or is everybody pretty miserable?"
Them: "Oh no, it's a wonderful place. Everyone loves it here — my daughter cries when the season ends."
That second sentence is your whole promo video. You would not have got it from the polite version of the question.

What failure sounds like — the hostage video. Everyone answers "yes, I love [Studio], it's a wonderful culture" — flat, agreeable, useless, and you don't find out until the edit. If the first two people give you that, change the question, not the person.

Keep takes shortThirty usable seconds per person is a season of posts.
Cut the top and tailThey warm up in the first sentence and wrap up in the last. Both are dead.
Let the silence sitThe pause after they finish is where the real answer arrives.
Captions, alwaysEvery talking-head clip gets animated captions before you post it.

Cut four hours of interviews in an evening FREE TOOLS

DaVinci Resolve is free, and it's what the pros use. Transcribe the whole shoot with its AI transcription, copy the wall of text into ChatGPT and ask: "four 60-second scripts from the best material in here, verbatim, nothing added." It hands the exact words back — search that text inside Resolve and it jumps you to the clip. CapCut is fine too, and easier for captions.

Run the whole content day — one morning, twelve months of video

Four stations at once: interview, selfie studio, routines, and the weird angles. Hair, makeup, costumes and lights happen once — and you walk out with your homepage film, your background video, your recital reels and dozens of social cuts.
