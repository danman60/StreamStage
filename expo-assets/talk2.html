A VERY SHORT HISTORY (0:14–0:16) · Slide 8

For fifty years, computers made you learn their language. Commands. Exact file names. The right button in the right menu. One character wrong and it just says no. Everybody in this room has felt stupid in front of a computer, and it was never your fault.

Then they started learning ours, one sense at a time. Spellcheck. Autocorrect. Your phone finding your daughter's face in four thousand photos. "Okay Google, take me home." Nobody called that AI. It just stopped making you do the work.

And now they've learned the whole thing: plain English. The most powerful software ever built ships with a text box and no manual. The breakthrough isn't the intelligence — it's the interface.

[CLICK] You feel like you're behind. You are not behind. This is the biggest shift since the internet — since the printing press. We are all very new at it.

[CLICK] And by the way: 99% of the time somebody says "AI", they mean ChatGPT, Claude or Perplexity. That's the whole vocabulary.

Pivot: which means the fastest way in isn't typing carefully — it's talking.

101 — WHAT IS THIS THING? (0:16–0:19) · Slide 9

It read almost everything ever written, and it predicts what comes next. That's it. Then it learned to search the web too, which helps and doesn't fix it.

The magic: anything made of language. Write it, rewrite it, shorten it, sort it, put it in your voice. That's most of your admin week.

The catch: it can be confidently wrong.

[CLICK] My favourite: you ask it, "should I walk to the car wash or drive? It's only ten minutes away." And it says: "just walk — it's so close!" (let them laugh)

[CLICK] So: trust, but verify. Check it before you hit send. You are the editor, always. (Backup joke if the room is flat: ask it how many R's are in "strawberry".)

101 — WHICH ONE DO I OPEN? (0:19–0:22) · Slide 10

ChatGPT is the all-rounder — start there, the phone app is the one you'll actually use. Claude for long documents and writing that sounds like you. NotebookLM (free, Google) to build a brain out of your own material. Gemini because it lives where your Google mail and drive already are. Nano Banana, inside Gemini, is the image one — and it writes text into the picture. Perplexity is the research one: live web, with sources.

Pro tip: keep a free account on all of them and cycle through as you run out. Just keep your documents in one folder so it doesn't matter which one you opened — that was literally my own early workflow.

I personally pay five, six hundred dollars a month for AI, because it's making me money. Everything in this talk runs on the free tiers.

(Don't paste anything you wouldn't put in an email — student names, medical, payment details.)

101 — THE ONLY SEVEN WORDS (0:22–0:25) · Slide 11 — the pyramid

Top of the pyramid down: Memory is what it knows about you — tell it who you are, once, and it applies everywhere. A project is a folder of threads sharing your studio's material. A thread is one conversation that remembers itself — new job, new thread, don't cross your contexts. A prompt is what you actually ask.

Then three more: a connector lets it reach your email and calendar. A skill is a job you teach it once and reuse. And an agent is something using tools on its own — it works while you sleep. As a business owner, you know the feeling I mean: you go to bed, you wake up, and something has already changed. That's what we want.

SEGMENT 1 — THE FIVE FREE MOVES (0:25–0:53)

(Delivery rule: name the pain → show the real thing on screen → one-sentence Monday move. Number them out loud — "free move number three" — so the structure stays audible for an hour.)

MOVE 1 — Talk, don't type (0:25–0:30) · Slide 12

I've been working with this stuff for about a year and a half, same as everybody. The single biggest thing that sped me up wasn't a model or a plugin. It was a button: the microphone.

We have something in common — studio owners love to talk. And you think differently when you talk than when you type. I type fast, I play piano, and I still talk to my robots all day, because things come out mid-sentence that never would have made it through my fingers.

To be clear: not voice mode, where it talks back. I read faster than the robot talks — that's for driving. I mean voice typing: the little mic on your phone keyboard, the dictation key on your laptop.

[LIVE / CLICK ×4] Watch. (Hit the mic and ramble a real Monday:) "Okay so costume payments are late again and I need to remind parents without sounding annoyed for the third time… and Tuesday ballet isn't filling like Thursday tap… book the photographer, call StreamStage about video… and I need a poster, fun and vibrant, warm and welcoming." Ramble. Ramble. Ramble.

Same trick, bigger: pull your Zoom transcripts off zoom.us and paste them in. Hit record on the teacher meeting. Record yourself in the car. People will sell you a little AI note pin — you already own a phone.

MONDAY: app on your first screen, mic every time.

MOVE 2 — Your studio in two files (0:30–0:35) · Slide 13

Here's the problem with AI writing: it sounds like nobody.

So give it two files. A voice file — how you talk, how you sign off, how you handle a sensitive email, the words you use and the words you don't. (Always dancers — never "kids".) And tell it to stop using em dashes. That one line fixes the most obvious tell there is.

A brand file — your colours, your fonts, your logo rules, the feel: warm, vibrant, welcoming. What your studio is, and what it isn't.

These are plain text one-pagers you own. Not a setting inside somebody's app. ChatGPT today, Claude next month, whatever exists in 2031 — the value is in your files.

How do you fill the voice file honestly? If you're on Gmail, Google Takeout exports all of your email. Hand it the real thing. Or drop fifty old newsletters into NotebookLM — it's free, it's Google's, you upload a pile of documents and it builds a knowledge base out of them — and ask, "what patterns do you see in how I write?" Then the monthly studio email is: "here's what we have — pajama day Friday, the twelve-year-old Acro hair moved from a messy bun to a high pony" — and out it comes, in your voice. Ten minutes instead of two hours.

MONDAY: start both files. One page each. Boring is fine.

THE FOLDER (0:35–0:37) · Slide 14

Those files don't float loose. They live in one folder. Call it "Studio Brain", or honestly just "AI stuff". In it: the voice file, the brand file, the handbook, last year's newsletters, key dates.

[CLICK] Step one is the copy-paste era — everything in one place so you always know where to grab it from. That was literally my workflow. [CLICK] Step two, the drag era — new chat, drag the two or three files that matter in. Ten seconds. (Do the gesture.) [CLICK] Step three, the connected era — you point it at the folder once and stop dragging.

You're building the thing you'll point every tool at for the next five years.

MOVE 3 — Make it write the prompt (0:37–0:42) · Slide 15

Everybody's real problem is "I don't know what to type."

So stop writing the prompt. Ask it to write the prompt.

[CLICK] "I need a poster for our June recital. Write me the prompt to make that image — ask me anything you need first."

[CLICK] And it hands you back the careful version: vertical 18×24, deep plum, soft warm spotlight upper left, one silhouetted dancer mid-leap, negative space in the bottom third for text, elegant not glittery, include this text in a modern serif…

[CLICK] You could never have typed that. You didn't have to. You're not the prompt engineer — it is. You just edit its prompt. That's the entire skill.

Related habit: ask how questions, not just do-this questions. "How would I go about doing this?" Half the time you don't know what "this" even is yet.

[CLICK] And it writes your two files as well: "read my website and my last ten newsletters, then write me a brand file and a voice file." You hand-author nothing. You just correct it.

MONDAY: next bad answer — go fix the prompt, not the answer.

MOVE 4 — Make the poster (0:42–0:47) · Slide 16 · [LIVE if wifi holds]

Nano Banana lives inside Gemini. ChatGPT makes images too. Both free tiers.

The thing that changed this year: it can put the text inside the image. Which is the whole difference between a picture and a poster.

[CLICK] Here's that prompt with no brand file. And look — it's lovely. Art nouveau, coral and teal, ribbons, the whole thing. I'd put that on a wall.

Now read the bottom of it. "Rhythm & Bloom Dance Studio presents…" That studio does not exist.

It didn't make a bad poster. It made somebody else's studio.

[CLICK] Same prompt, plus the brand file from Move 2. Your sage, your serif, your dancers, your negative space. Same four minutes.

That is what those two files buy you — and it's why Move 2 is worth the twenty minutes. When people say AI posters look generic, it isn't the model. It's that nobody ever told it who they were.

[CLICK] And it does the rest of your year too: fall registration, the carousel card for picture day, class-schedule graphics, costume handouts.

Recital posters, social carousels, class-schedule graphics, costume handouts. You don't need to be a designer. You need taste — and you already know which one of those is better.

MONDAY: make the one poster you were going to pay somebody for.

SKILLS + THE DARK VALLEY (0:47–0:49) · Slide 17

Two rules that make everything else stick.

One: if you catch yourself correcting the same thing — "that's not my voice, say it like this" — stop correcting and teach it once. Some tools call that a skill.

Two, the honest math. [CLICK] The task takes 10 minutes, every time you do it. [CLICK] Automating it takes 30 minutes — once. [CLICK] After that it takes 2 minutes, forever.

[CLICK] So on day one you lose twenty minutes. That's the dark valley. Everybody hits it, and it's exactly where most people quit. Push through on the one thing you do most often.

And for a business owner: saving time is the same as making money. It frees up the attention you spend on the work that actually earns.

MOVE 5 — Ask your own handbook ★ (0:49–0:53) · Slide 18 — SLOW DOWN

Upload the handbook. All your rules, your hours, your dress code, your key dates, tuition.

[CLICK] "What's the hair for senior jazz on Thursday?" — High pony, no bangs, page 12. [CLICK] "What costume for musical theatre senior on Saturdays?" — Costume B, character shoes, page 14.

Notice those are staff questions. Teachers, subs, your September hire. Move 2 taught it your voice; this one teaches it your rules. Same folder, and now everybody who works for you can ask it instead of asking you.

Then ask it this one: "does this handbook contradict itself about refunds?" It will find it.

[CLICK] And… wouldn't it be cool if that handbook — and all your latest info — lived somewhere public? (Don't answer that. Move on.)

MONDAY: upload it, ask the three questions your staff ask most.

WHERE IT SHOULD NOT REPLACE YOU (0:53–0:55) · Slide 19 — ⚠ published promise, do not cut

Hand over the repeats: "what time is Saturday", the newsletter draft, the schedule first pass, the link they already have, the reminder nobody read.

Never hand over the conversations. The upset parent. Placement and casting. An injury. Anything about a dancer's body, ability or confidence. The apology when you got it wrong.

If it changes how a family feels about their dancer — that's you. Always.

[CLICK] And here's the number that makes this workable: about 80% of the conversations you're having are the repeats. On those, accuracy and speed matter more than tact. On the hard ones it still helps you — get it to review your draft, or role-play the difficult conversation in a thread first, and then go have it like a human being.

[CLICK] Five moves, one move: hand it your material once, and stop repeating yourself.

[CLICK] First we talked to each other. Then somebody made a hammer. Then a screwdriver. Tools are the whole difference — that's the entire word "agentic".

"Every weekday at 7am — brief me."

RUNG 3 — it goes and gets it (1:03–1:05) · Slide 25

Everything up to here, you fed it by hand. Connectors are when it goes and gets the information.

"Read my teachers' emails, find the conflicts, and build next week."

[CLICK] Before you flinch: it's read-only, and you approve anything it sends. [CLICK] Ninety seconds to a draft beats a Sunday night.

Notice what just changed — it went and got something.

BE WITH PEOPLE, NOT INTERFACES (1:05–1:07) · Slide 26

Here's why anybody should want this.

We want things happening while we're not doing them. [CLICK] We want to leverage the thing only you have — your taste. The machine has no opinion about your studio; you do. [CLICK] People are expensive and they need training, and nobody was hired to retype the same answer — get your people doing what they actually want to do. [CLICK] And where it goes: my agent talks to your agent, finds the Tuesday, and books the coffee. Nobody should be trading six emails to find an hour.

What we should be doing is being with people. Not with data. Not with interfaces. Not with buttons and knobs and sliders.

ROUTINE 436 (1:07–1:09) · Slide 27 — emotional peak, slow down

Here's what I tell my operators.

For us, it's routine 436 on the Saturday. Just another number on a long day.

For that dancer, it's the most important three minutes of their season. It might be the only competition they dance all year.

So we do not look away. And we do not mess it up.

THE LOOP PAYOFF (1:09–1:12) · Slide 28

Mid-competition. Eight cameras — wide, close-up, side stage, crowd. My email is filling up while I'm watching angles. And in comes: "I never got the link."

Spoiler: they got the link. They did, though. We emailed it to them.

So I built a little email spider. [CLICK] It catches the email, checks the calendar for which event I'm actually at, drafts the reply, and scores its own confidence. 85% or above, it fires. Below that, it asks me. Then it pings me either way.

[CLICK] I never touched my keyboard.

[CLICK] That's agentic. It's taking action, it's using tools — on a workload I designed and supervised. I managed the first few myself.

PULL, DON'T PUSH (1:12–1:15) · Slide 29

We push it, and push it, and push it, and they never seem to have it — because at the moment you pushed, they didn't want it yet.

Let me indict myself. I'm a dance parent now. [CLICK] Five years old, unicorn hat, Pink Pony Club, her very first recital. [CLICK] (photo takes the screen — stop talking for a second)

I work in tech, and I still couldn't find the email.

So what do parents actually do? They search their inbox, they can't find it — Outlook search is terrible — and then they text you.

Push loses. Pull is when they're ready, and that's when it sticks. That's why I built StudioSage.

The demo studio keeps its 15 hand-seeded facts so the audience-texting portion always works. Forward a studio newsletter or announcement, NOT a parent conversation. A forwarded parent thread can drop real names/phone numbers into the KB and onto the projector wall. Newsletters are safe.
